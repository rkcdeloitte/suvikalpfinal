Section One

What is the GCF, and who can actually get it?

The Green Climate Fund (GCF) is the world's largest dedicated multilateral climate fund, established in 2010 as a financial mechanism of the UNFCCC and operating under the guidance of the Paris Agreement. It funds both mitigation (reducing/avoiding emissions) and adaptation (building resilience) in developing countries, targeting a 50:50 balance between the two.

Critical reality check You — as an individual, company, NGO, or state department — cannot apply to the GCF directly. GCF channels every dollar through an Accredited Entity (AE): an organisation that has gone through GCF's own due-diligence accreditation. If you're not accredited yourself, you partner with one that is, and it submits the proposal on your behalf while you act as the Executing Entity implementing on the ground.

How the money actually flows

1
GCF Board & Secretariat

Approves funding proposals; holds the resource pool (~USD 9–15bn+ in confirmed pledges across replenishment cycles).

2
Accredited Entities (AE)

National, regional or international bodies — public, private or non-governmental — cleared to develop, submit and manage GCF-funded activity.

3
Executing Entities / Project Proponents

Implement the approved project or programme on the ground, usually under a sub-agreement with the AE — this is most likely you.

4
Financial instruments deployed

Grants, concessional/senior/subordinated loans, equity, guarantees and results-based payments — often blended together.

Every step above is shadowed by India's National Designated Authority (NDA) — MoEFCC — which must confirm country ownership before anything reaches the GCF Board.

Section Two

The funding tracks — and which one is yours

GCF offers several access mechanisms. Picking the right one on day one saves months. Use the quick decision path below, then the full comparison table.

Quick decision path

A
Is your GCF ask ≤ USD 10 million AND minimal/no environmental-social risk (Category C)?

→ Use the Simplified Approval Process (SAP). Faster, lighter documentation, same six criteria.

B
Not accredited, but you have one strong, ready project rather than a pipeline?

→ Use the Project-Specific Assessment Approach (PSAA) — project-level screening, no institutional accreditation needed.

C
Concept is good but not yet "fundable" — needs studies, ESIA, financial structuring?

→ Request Project Preparation Facility (PPF) support (up to USD 1.5m) through your AE, or ask MoEFCC about Readiness support first.

D
Everything else — larger, more complex, or higher-risk (Category A/B) proposals

→ Use the Regular / Full Funding Proposal track (PAP) — no upper size cap, but heavier documentation and longer review.

On co-financing GCF strongly prefers it isn't the only money in a project. Industry norm is GCF covering roughly 20–40% of total project cost, the rest from government grants, bank loans, state budgets or your own equity. A co-financing ratio of 3:1 or better (₹3 from elsewhere for every ₹1 from GCF) meaningfully strengthens a proposal — though some approved India projects (e.g. the Odisha groundwater programme) had GCF covering as little as ~21% of total cost, so there is no fixed rule, only a directional preference.

Section Three

The six investment criteria — the core of eligibility

Every proposal, regardless of size, sector or track, is scored against these six criteria by GCF's Independent Technical Advisory Panel (iTAP). There's no published pass mark — but a weak showing on even one criterion is a common cause of rejection or repeated revision cycles.

1 · Impact potential

Mitigation: tonnes of CO₂-equivalent avoided or sequestered over the project lifetime, calculated (not estimated) using a recognised methodology (IPCC, Verra VCS, Gold Standard). Adaptation: number of direct + indirect beneficiaries and the measurable reduction in climate vulnerability.

2 · Paradigm-shift potential

Can this change a market, sector or policy landscape beyond itself? Will it be replicated, crowd in private finance, or shift institutional behaviour at scale? A one-off project with no scale-up plan scores poorly here.

3 · Sustainable development potential

Co-benefits beyond climate: economic (jobs, incomes), social (gender equity, poverty reduction), environmental (biodiversity, water, air) and health. A substantive gender co-benefit is specifically expected, not optional.

4 · Needs of the recipient

Why does this population or geography need GCF money specifically? What financial, institutional or technical barrier prevents it happening without GCF — i.e., what is the market failure or financing gap you are closing?

5 · Country ownership

Explicit alignment with India's NDC, NAPCC, relevant State Action Plan (SAPCC) and sectoral missions; genuine stakeholder consultation; and — non-negotiably — the NDA's No-Objection Letter.

6 · Efficiency & effectiveness

Economic/financial soundness — IRR, NPV or payback period — plus the headline metric, cost per tonne of CO₂ avoided, and the co-financing ratio mobilised alongside GCF's contribution.

Efficiency benchmarks worth knowing

MetricComfortable rangeWill draw scrutiny
Cost per tonne CO₂e (general)USD 5–30 / tonneUSD 100+ / tonne without strong adaptation co-benefits framing
Co-financing share (GCF as % of total cost)~20–40% of total project costGCF funding the near-entirety of project cost, with no clear justification
FormulaCost per tonne = Total GCF funding (USD) ÷ Total lifetime CO₂e avoided or sequestered (tonnes)

GCF does not publish a minimum tonnage or a hard efficiency threshold — what matters is that your claimed impact is credible, methodologically sound, and proportionate to what you're asking for. Use the calculator in Section 11 to check your own numbers.

Section Four

India's NDC — exactly what "country ownership" means

India's Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) is your project's policy passport. Without an explicit, specific link to it, a proposal fails the "country ownership" criterion outright — a generic sentence ("this aligns with India's NDC") is not enough.

TargetCommitmentApplies to
Emissions intensityReduce GDP emissions intensity by 45% from 2005 levels by 2030Every proposal — reference this regardless of sector
Non-fossil power capacity50% of installed electricity capacity from non-fossil sources by 2030 (COP26 "Panchamrit": 500 GW non-fossil capacity by 2030)Energy-generation and grid-linked projects
Carbon sinkAdditional carbon sink of 2.5–3 billion tonnes CO₂e through forest and tree cover by 2030Forestry, land-use, agroforestry, ecosystem/conservation projects
Net zeroNet zero emissions by 2070Long-term framing context for any proposal

How to actually write the alignment section

Template language "This project directly contributes to India's Updated NDC target of [specific target] by [mechanism]. By [core project activity] in [state/district], the project is expected to [quantified contribution] toward the national target, and aligns with [relevant national policy] and the [State]'s State Action Plan on Climate Change (SAPCC)."

National policy documents to reference

National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC)

Eight national missions spanning solar, energy efficiency, water, agriculture, the Himalayan ecosystem, sustainable habitat, Green India, and strategic knowledge — cite whichever mission your project sits under.

State Action Plan on Climate Change (SAPCC)

Every state has one. Explicitly citing your state's SAPCC and how your project delivers one of its stated priorities is a strong sub-national country-ownership signal — available on MoEFCC's website.

Mission LiFE

"Lifestyle for Environment" — India's non-quantitative NDC commitment on everyday individual climate action. Useful framing for community-facing or behaviour-change components.

National Adaptation Fund for Climate Change (NAFCC)

India's domestic adaptation financing vehicle — reference where your project has adaptation/resilience components complementary to NAFCC-funded work.

Long-Term Low Emission Development Strategy (LT-LEDS)

India's seven key transitions toward 2070 net zero — submitted at COP27. Useful for framing long-horizon, transformational projects.

Sector-specific missions/schemes

e.g. National Solar Mission, PM-KUSUM, National Agroforestry Policy, National Mission for a Green India, Integrated Coastal Zone Management — cite whichever is directly relevant to your sector.

Sources: India's Updated NDC (UNFCCC submission, 2022) · India's LT-LEDS (COP27) · MoEFCC SAPCC repository

Step-by-step application process

The overall journey is the same shape regardless of track — only the depth of documentation and review time changes. Expand the track relevant to you below for the exact sequence.

The journey, idea to disbursement

1
Identify a project aligned with India's NDC & national priorities
2
Approach an Accredited Entity active in India, or the NDA (MoEFCC), for early guidance
3
Jointly check alignment with the 6 investment criteria & pick the right track (Regular / SAP / PSAA)
4
Develop & submit a Concept Note for early Secretariat feedback (optional but strongly recommended; mandatory before any PPF request)
5
MoEFCC issues the No-Objection Letter confirming country ownership
6
AE develops and submits the full Funding Proposal via the GCF Partner Portal
7
GCF Secretariat appraisal + independent Technical Advisory Panel (iTAP) review
8
GCF Board considers and decides — approve / approve with conditions / reject
9
Legal arrangements: Funded Activity Agreement signed; first disbursement released
10
Implementation, monitoring, reporting and eventual project closure

Section Eight

Why proposals get rejected — and how to avoid it

These patterns recur across sectors and tracks. Screen your own proposal against every row before submission.

Common reasonHow to avoid it
Weak additionality — "this would happen anyway"Prove the financing gap with data: show commercial lenders won't fund it, or that the cost of capital without GCF is prohibitive.
No paradigm-shift potential — one-off project, no replication planInclude a genuine theory of change: how the project transforms the sector, catalyses others, or shifts policy. Add a specific scale-up plan.
Poor impact methodology — vague or unverifiable claimsUse a recognised methodology (IPCC, Verra VCS, Gold Standard) and show your calculation explicitly. Engage a climate-finance consultant if needed.
Missing or weak gender componentBuild in a gender assessment from the start, with sex-disaggregated data and concrete inclusion actions — not an afterthought section.
No NDA No-Objection LetterEngage MoEFCC early. This is non-negotiable — a proposal without it is automatically incomplete.
High cost per tonne of CO₂Revisit scale and design; larger scale generally improves efficiency. If cost/tonne exceeds ~USD 50–100, strengthen adaptation co-benefits framing or redesign.
Weak stakeholder engagementDocument real, meaningful consultation — minutes, attendance records, community sign-off. Tokenistic consultation is penalised by the iTAP.
Doubts about fund-management capacityEnsure the executing entity has audited accounts, sound financial/HR systems, and a track record managing grants — your AE will conduct due diligence on this.
Compiled from GCF Board decision patterns, iTAP guidance and India NDA/AE practitioner records.

Readiness & Preparatory Support

Direct Accreditation

~9-month Accreditation Panel review after completeness check, plus Board scheduling (Board meets ~3 times a year) and Accreditation Master Agreement signature.

Reality check These are optimistic, best-case estimates. Many real projects take 3–5 years from concept to first disbursement, with multiple revision cycles along the way. Line up other funding sources to sustain the project during this period — do not make it entirely dependent on GCF materialising by a fixed date.

Section Ten

Governing rules, regulations & India's clearance structure

India's institutional chain

1
National Designated Authority — MoEFCC (Secretary level)

Policy guidance and final sign-off on country ownership.

2
GCF Empowered Committee (GCF-EC) & GCF Focal Point, Climate Change Division

GCF-EC (chaired by the MoEFCC Secretary, 16+ members drawn from MoEFCC, MEA, Finance, Power, MNRE, Housing & Urban Affairs, Agriculture, Earth Sciences, Jal Shakti, Health, Science & Technology and NITI Aayog) determines national priorities, sets DAE/project eligibility criteria, and approves India's GCF projects.

3
Climate Change Division, MoEFCC

Day-to-day secretariat function — processes Concept Notes, coordinates GCF-EC review, and drafts the No-Objection Letter.

How a No-Objection Letter (NOL) is issued

1
Concept Note submitted to the NDA through the AE/DAE
2
CN circulated to GCF-EC members for comments/feedback
3
Comments shared with the AE/DAE; CN revised and resubmitted
4
CN reviewed in a GCF-EC meeting chaired by the MoEFCC Secretary
5
If approved — SAP/PPF proposals receive the NOL on the Concept Note directly (mandatory); other proposals proceed to Funding Proposal stage, verified against the CN, then NOL issued

If not approved, a revised CN can be resubmitted after incorporating GCF-EC comments.

Documents required for an NOL

  • · Concept Note (GCF template)
  • · Recommendation of the Executing Entity
  • · Approval of the State Level Steering Committee, if applicable
